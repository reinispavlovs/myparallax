Source extract (attributed)

The supplied source extract is titled, in substance, as a discussion of cosmogenic nuclide enhancement via deposition from long-period comets as a test of the Younger Dryas impact hypothesis. The study reports that it explores the idea that detectable excursions in 26Al may arise from direct deposition by any bolide, and that excursions in 14C and 10Be abundances in the atmosphere may result from long-period comet impacts. The extract states that this is very different from the usual processes of production by cosmic ray — the supplied text ends at that point.

Classification of this extract: suggestive as a proposed test, not established as a completed demonstration. The source explores a possible observational path; this draft does not treat the Younger Dryas impact hypothesis as shown or as a closed question.

Independent evaluation of evidence categories

Illustrative orbital cycle and flux

The dataset assigns a cycle of 25920 years and an illustrative orbital flux peak of 100.0, with boundary status CYCLE_SYNCHRONIZED. Because the model note states that this is a sinusoidal illustration and not a geological measurement, these figures are classified as unsubstantiated as empirical orbital or stratigraphic measurements. They may be used only as a schematic timeline. No measured orbital eccentricity value appears in the dataset; claims that eccentricity itself drives a catastrophe cycle are therefore unresolved on this record and are not filled in with outside figures.

Younger Dryas marker

The supplied marker is 12800 BP. That figure is the draft’s reference marker only. Alignment of that marker with the 25920-year illustration, including the CYCLE_SYNCHRONIZED label, is unresolved as a physical mechanism. The cautionary check states that the mathematical cyclical model is illustrative and is not an established correlation mechanism.

Impact-related sediment marker, kept separate from the cycle

The cautionary check states that the sediment platinum peak (~12,800 BP) indicates an external impact event, and that this is a separate issue from the orbital mechanics cycle. This draft follows that separation. An external-impact reading of a platinum peak is treated as supported only as the content of that cautionary check, not as a result derived from the sinusoidal model. Combining the platinum peak and the 25920-year illustration into one conclusion is not done here.

Cosmogenic nuclides and long-period comets

Direct bolide deposition as a possible source of 26Al excursions, and long-period comet impacts as a possible source of atmospheric 14C and 10Be excursions, are classified as suggestive research ideas attributed to the found source extract. They are not classified as established production pathways on the basis of this dataset alone. The extract itself contrasts those ideas with usual cosmic-ray production; that contrast is reported, not enlarged.

Tribunal notes on non-conflation

Project Parallax does not collapse a non-mainstream cyclical reading into a void solely because a consensus mechanism is absent. It also does not treat a model label such as CYCLE_SYNCHRONIZED as truth. Two questions remain distinct:

  • Whether an external impact-related signal near the supplied Younger Dryas marker is a live empirical issue (the cautionary check points to a platinum peak at ~12,800 BP as indicating an external impact event).
  • Whether a 25920-year orbital illustration, with an illustrative flux peak of 100.0, is a causal cycle for that signal (not established on this dataset; classified unresolved / illustrative only).

Orbital eccentricity as a named driver is not quantified in the supplied record. Any later version of this draft would need literature-sourced eccentricity and flux series, separately audited, before a supported claim could be considered. Until then, cyclical catastrophism linked to eccentricity remains an open framing, not a measured result.
